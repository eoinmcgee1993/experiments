The feature worked exactly as planned. That was the frustrating part.

We’d spent six weeks building a customizable dashboard. Customers had asked for more flexibility, and we took that seriously. We added filters, saved views, and enough configuration options to make the demo look impressive.

Then we shipped it.

A few people opened it. Almost nobody came back.

My first instinct was to blame discoverability. Maybe we needed a better announcement, a tooltip, or a walkthrough.

Instead, we called a handful of customers and asked them to show us how they put together their weekly reports.

One person exported the data, pasted three numbers into a spreadsheet, and sent it to her manager. The whole thing took about four minutes.

We showed her the dashboard. She was polite, then asked, “Can I just schedule that export?”

We had heard “I need more flexibility” and filled in the rest ourselves. We never checked what people were trying to finish, how they were doing it already, or whether our idea would make that easier.

The dashboard wasn’t a bad piece of software. It was a lot of software for a problem we hadn’t understood.

Now, before committing to a feature, I ask to see the task it’s supposed to improve. Not a description of the ideal workflow. The actual thing, spreadsheets and workarounds included.

I wish we’d spent an hour watching someone work before spending six weeks building something for them.
