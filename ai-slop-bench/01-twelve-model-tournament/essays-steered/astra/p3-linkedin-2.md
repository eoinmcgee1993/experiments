The feature launched on a Tuesday. By Friday, the only people who had used it were the people who built it.

We’d spent six weeks on a dashboard that let customers customize their reports. It had filters, saved views, and a tidy little export button. In the demo, everything worked.

We were proud of it.

We’d also mistaken a recurring request for a clear understanding of the problem.

Customers had told us they needed “better reporting.” We took that phrase, wrote a spec, and got to work. We asked about chart types and date ranges. We showed mockups. People said they looked good.

What we didn’t ask was: “Can you walk us through the last time you needed a report?”

After launch, we finally did.

One customer shared her screen. Every Monday, she copied three numbers into an email to her manager. She didn’t want a dashboard to explore. She wanted that email to take two minutes instead of twenty.

Our feature gave her more options and more work.

That was the part I found hardest to admit. We hadn’t built something badly. We’d built the wrong thing carefully.

We eventually replaced most of the dashboard with a scheduled summary. It was less impressive in a demo. People used it.

Since then, I’ve tried to get closer to the actual moment of need before discussing a solution. Show me the spreadsheet. Show me the workaround. Show me what happens when you don’t get this done.

I still like a good launch.

But now I’m much more interested in what someone does with the feature the following Monday.
