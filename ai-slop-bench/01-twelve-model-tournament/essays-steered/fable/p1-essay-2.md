Most product roadmaps fail because they are asked to do too many jobs at once. A single document is supposed to reassure the board, motivate engineers, keep sales from overpromising, and give customers a reason to renew. Those audiences want different things, and a roadmap that tries to please all of them ends up as a list of features with dates attached, which is the one form guaranteed to disappoint everyone.

The dates are the first problem. Software estimation is famously bad, but roadmaps treat it as if it were reliable enough to publish a year out. Someone picks a quarter for a feature because the slide needs a quarter, and that guess hardens into a commitment the moment a salesperson repeats it to a prospect. When the date slips, and it almost always slips, the team looks incompetent even if they made the right call to spend those weeks on something more urgent. The roadmap becomes a record of broken promises rather than a description of intent.

The second problem is that roadmaps are usually built from the inside out. They start with what the company wants to ship, not with what the customer is struggling to do. A feature gets on the list because an executive likes it, or a big account asked for it, or a competitor announced it last month. None of those are terrible reasons on their own, but stacked together they produce a plan with no center of gravity. Nobody can say what the product is trying to become, only what it will contain. And a plan with no thesis cannot tell you what to cut when things get tight, so nothing gets cut and everything ships late and half finished.

Third, roadmaps assume the world will hold still. They are written with the information available in January and then defended through December as if nothing was learned in between. In reality a good team learns something important every few weeks, from support tickets, usage data, a lost deal, a competitor stumbling. The healthy response is to change course. But changing the roadmap feels like admitting the original was wrong, so teams either quietly ignore it or spend energy negotiating amendments instead of building. Either way the document stops reflecting what people actually do.

There is also a quieter failure, which is that the roadmap gets mistaken for the strategy. Writing down a sequence of deliverables feels like thinking, but it is not the same as deciding who you are for, what you will refuse to build, and what you believe about where the market is heading. Without that layer, the roadmap is just a to do list wearing a suit.

The roadmaps that work tend to be humbler. They commit firmly to the near term, sketch the middle distance in terms of problems rather than features, and leave the far horizon deliberately vague. They say why before they say what. And they are treated as a snapshot of current thinking, revised without shame, rather than a contract to be enforced. That is less satisfying to present in a meeting, but it has the advantage of being true.
