Most product roadmaps fail because they are asked to do two jobs at once, and those jobs pull in opposite directions. The first job is to help a team think clearly about what matters and in what order. The second is to make a promise to the rest of the company, to customers, to the board, that certain things will exist by certain dates. A document that is good at the first job has to stay loose, because thinking changes as you learn. A document that is good at the second job has to stay fixed, because promises lose their value when they move. Nobody wants to choose, so the roadmap tries to be both and ends up being neither.

The trouble starts early. Someone builds a slide with a timeline and puts features on it. The features come from a mix of sources: a large customer who threatened to leave, a competitor who shipped something scary, an executive who has been talking about an idea for a year, and a handful of things the engineers actually think are important. There is rarely a shared theory of why these particular items will move the business. They are just the things that were loud at the time the slide was made. When you ask what problem the roadmap is solving, you tend to get a list of outputs rather than an answer.

Then the roadmap meets reality. Estimates were optimistic, as they always are. A dependency on another team turns out to be more complicated. A customer interview reveals that the thing everyone was excited about does not solve the problem people actually have. At this point a healthy team would revise the plan. But the roadmap has already been shown to sales, who have already mentioned it to prospects, who have already put it in their own plans. So instead of changing the roadmap, the team changes the definition of done. The feature ships in a diminished form, it gets marked green, and the fiction holds for another quarter.

There is also a quieter failure that gets less attention. Roadmaps tend to be written by people who are far from the work and read by people who are even farther from it. The person who understands the tradeoffs in detail is usually not the person deciding what goes on the slide, and the person approving the slide often could not describe how any single item would be built or measured. The document becomes a kind of ceremony, a way for the organization to feel aligned without doing the harder work of actually agreeing on what success looks like.

Roadmaps that work tend to look less impressive. They talk about problems rather than features, they get vaguer the farther out they go, and they are revisited often enough that nobody is shocked when something moves. They are honest about uncertainty instead of hiding it in a Gantt chart. That honesty is uncomfortable, especially in companies where confidence is rewarded more than accuracy. But a roadmap that cannot survive contact with new information was never really a plan. It was a wish list with dates on it, and wish lists have a poor record of coming true.
