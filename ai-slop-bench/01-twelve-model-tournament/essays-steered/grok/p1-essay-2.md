I've watched more product roadmaps collapse than I can count, and it usually isn't because the ideas were bad. The real problem is that most teams treat the roadmap like a contract instead of a hypothesis. They lock in features and dates months ahead based on incomplete information, then act surprised when customers don't care, technology changes, or a competitor ships something better. Updating it feels like admitting failure, so the document slowly becomes fiction while the actual product evolves in a completely different direction.

A lot of this comes from confusing outputs with outcomes. The typical roadmap is a list of things to build, not a set of problems to solve or results to achieve. Stakeholders demand their features appear on it, product managers add them to keep the peace, and suddenly the team is building a Frankenstein of requests instead of a coherent product. There's almost never enough time spent talking to users before those items get committed. Assumptions stay assumptions until it's too late and expensive to reverse.

Capacity is another silent killer. Roadmaps rarely account for the messy reality of software: bugs, technical debt, onboarding, support, the unexpected production incident. Everything is estimated as if the team will operate at peak efficiency forever. When dates inevitably slip, leadership loses faith, engineering feels set up to fail, and the next planning cycle starts with even more cynicism.

Politics make it worse. The person with the most power or the loudest voice often dictates what goes on the page, regardless of data. Strategy gets diluted into a collection of unrelated bets. People also mix up a roadmap with a detailed project plan and expect precision that discovery work simply cannot provide.

The roadmaps that survive are usually shorter, more flexible, focused on themes or customer problems rather than specific features, and treated as conversation starters rather than sacred texts. Most organizations want the appearance of certainty more than they want actual learning. That's why the majority fail. They become artifacts of wishful thinking instead of tools for making better decisions.
