I spent three months last year building a feature I was sure would change how our users worked. We had the data, or so I thought. Support tickets mentioned related pain points. A couple of power users had asked for something similar in passing. I sketched it, got buy-in, and we shipped it with a nice little announcement.

Then nothing happened. Adoption sat at basically zero. A handful of people clicked around once and never came back. I checked the analytics every morning for weeks like it would suddenly start moving.

The hardest part was admitting I had built something for myself, not for them. I had filled in the gaps with my own assumptions. I never sat down with five actual customers and watched them try to solve the problem. I never asked if they even considered it a problem worth solving.

What I learned is that enthusiasm is not evidence. A few loud voices are not a market. Shipping is not the finish line if nobody cares. Now I force myself to talk to users before we write a single line of code for anything non-trivial. I ask them to show me their current workaround. If they don't have one, maybe the problem isn't real enough.

It stung. It still stings a little when I think about the time we could have spent on something people actually wanted. But it made me slower in a good way. I'd rather ship less and have it matter.
