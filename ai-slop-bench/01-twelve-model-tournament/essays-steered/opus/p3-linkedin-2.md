We spent four months building a custom dashboard builder. Six weeks after launch, 1.8% of accounts had created a dashboard. Half of those were internal test accounts.

I still think about that number.

The feature wasn't badly built. The engineering was solid, the drag and drop worked, we shipped on time. It just turned out that almost nobody wanted to build a dashboard. They wanted to know one thing: whether the number they cared about was going up or down this week.

Here's where I went wrong, and it wasn't a research failure in the way you might expect. We did talk to customers. Nineteen of them, over three weeks. A lot of them asked for better reporting. Some of them said the words "custom dashboard" out loud, unprompted. I took that as validation and moved on.

What I didn't do was ask what they would stop doing once they had it. That question, which I now ask in every discovery conversation, would have saved us a quarter. Because the honest answer for most of those customers was nothing. They were already pulling a CSV into a spreadsheet once a month, it took twenty minutes, and it was fine. The pain was real but small, and small pain does not motivate anyone to learn a new tool.

The other thing I missed is that people are generous in interviews. When you ask someone what they want, they will help you. They will brainstorm with you. They will describe a feature because describing a feature feels more useful than saying "honestly, this isn't a big deal for me." That generosity is easy to mistake for demand.

Now I try to look for evidence that someone has already tried to solve the problem themselves. A spreadsheet held together with formulas nobody understands. A Zapier automation. A recurring calendar reminder. Somebody paying a contractor to do it manually. Those workarounds are expensive and awkward and they exist anyway, which tells you the pain is worth money. Nobody builds a workaround for a problem they don't have.

We eventually killed the dashboard builder and replaced it with a weekly email showing four metrics with an arrow next to each one. It took a sprint. Open rates sit around 60% and it comes up in sales calls constantly.

The lesson I keep relearning is that requests tell you where people are looking, not what they will do. The gap between those two things is where roadmaps go to die.

If you're about to commit a quarter to something, one question worth sitting with: what is the customer doing today instead, and how much does it actually cost them? If you can't answer that with specifics, you probably aren't ready to build yet.

I wasn't. Four months taught me that better than any framework could have.
