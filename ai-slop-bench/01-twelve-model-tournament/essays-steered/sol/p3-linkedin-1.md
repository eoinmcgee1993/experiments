We once spent weeks building a feature customers said they wanted.

The requests sounded clear. The idea fit our product strategy. The team designed it carefully, handled the edge cases, polished the experience, and launched it with real excitement.

Then almost nobody used it.

Our first reaction was to question the launch. Maybe customers had not seen the announcement. Maybe the feature was hard to find. Maybe we needed better onboarding.

Those things mattered, but they were not the real problem.

We had mistaken interest for urgency.

Customers liked the idea when we described it. They could imagine using it. Some even asked when it would be available. But the problem it solved was not painful enough to change their behavior.

The biggest lesson was that feature requests are not the same as evidence.

Now, before committing significant time, we look for signs of real effort. Are customers using a workaround? Are they spending money on another solution? Are they repeatedly raising the issue without being prompted? Will they try an imperfect version today?

We also try to test behavior instead of collecting opinions. A prototype, a manual service, or a simple button can teach us more than a long list of enthusiastic responses.

Shipping unused work was frustrating, but it changed how I think about product development.

The goal is not to build what people say sounds useful.

The goal is to understand what matters enough for them to act.
