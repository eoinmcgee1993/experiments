We once spent six weeks building a feature customers had asked for.

The request appeared in interviews, support tickets, and sales calls. It seemed obvious: people wanted more control, so we built a flexible set of options, added permissions, polished the interface, and announced the release.

Almost nobody used it.

At first, we blamed discoverability. We added tooltips, emails, and in-app prompts. Usage barely moved.

The real problem was simpler. We had listened to what customers said they wanted, but we had not understood the job they were trying to do. They did not actually want more control. They wanted to feel confident that the default behavior would work without creating problems.

We had built a better steering wheel for people who wanted a reliable train.

That experience changed how I think about customer requests. A request is evidence, not a specification. It points to friction, but it does not always identify the right solution.

Now, before committing to a feature, I try to ask a few more questions. What happened right before someone asked for this? What are they doing today instead? How often does the problem occur? What would improve if we solved it? Can we test the behavior with a manual process or a rough prototype first?

Shipping something nobody uses is painful, but it can be valuable if it improves the way you build.

The lesson was not to ignore customers. It was to listen more carefully, test assumptions earlier, and measure success by changed behavior rather than shipped code.
