We once spent weeks building a feature customers said they wanted.

The feedback was consistent. The use case sounded clear. The team designed it carefully, handled the edge cases, and shipped it on schedule.

Then almost nobody used it.

Our first instinct was to question the launch. Maybe people had not noticed it. Maybe the onboarding was unclear. Maybe we needed another announcement.

But when we spoke with customers, the real problem became obvious. They liked the idea of the feature, but it did not solve a problem that was urgent enough to change their behavior.

We had asked, “Would this be useful?” We should have asked, “What are you doing today to solve this problem?”

That distinction changed how I think about product discovery.

People are often positive about hypothetical solutions. It is easy to say a feature sounds useful. Existing behavior is much stronger evidence. If someone is already spending time, money, or effort on a workaround, there is probably a real problem to solve. If they are doing nothing, the problem may not matter as much as they say it does.

Now, before building, I look for proof of pain. I ask when the problem last happened, what the person did next, and what the consequences were. I also look for the smallest version we can test before making a larger investment.

Shipping a feature nobody used was frustrating, but it taught us something valuable: customer interest is not the same as customer demand.

Listen to what people say, but build based on what they do.
