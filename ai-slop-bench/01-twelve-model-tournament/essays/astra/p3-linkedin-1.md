The feature shipped on time. Nobody used it.

We’d done the work: polished the interface, handled edge cases, written documentation, and celebrated the release.

Then we watched the dashboard.

Almost nothing.

Our first instinct was to blame discoverability. Maybe it needed a better announcement. A tooltip. A more prominent button.

But after talking to customers, we found a harder truth:

We had validated that the idea sounded useful—not that the problem mattered enough for someone to change their behavior.

“Would you use this?” had produced encouraging answers.

“What do you do today when this happens?” revealed workarounds people were perfectly happy with.

And “When was the last time this caused a real problem?” often led to a long pause.

The lesson wasn’t “never build something new.” It was this:

**Interest is not intent. Shipping is not adoption.**

Since then, I’ve tried to make three things clear before committing to a feature:

• **The behavior we expect to change.** Not “users will have more flexibility,” but “users will stop exporting this report to a spreadsheet every Friday.”

• **The evidence that the problem matters.** Recent examples, repeated workarounds, and time or money lost—not just positive reactions to a mockup.

• **The smallest test that could prove us wrong.** A manual service, a rough prototype, or a limited pilot before a full build.

We also define what meaningful adoption would look like—and when we’ll review it. Otherwise, an unused feature can linger indefinitely because nobody wants to call it.

I’m still proud of how that team delivered. But we delivered a good answer to a question our customers weren’t really asking.

Now, I’d rather learn that in a week of discovery than a quarter of development.
