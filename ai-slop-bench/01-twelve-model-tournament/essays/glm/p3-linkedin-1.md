**We spent five months building a feature that almost nobody used.**

Not "slow adoption." Not "mixed reception." After 90 days, you could fit our weekly active users into one elevator.

Here's how it happened.

Reporting was the #1 request in every sales call. Prospects saw competitors' customizable dashboards and asked why we didn't have one. So we did the obvious thing: we built the best dashboard we could imagine. 20+ widgets. Drag-and-drop layouts. Saved views. Color-coded everything.

The internal demo got applause. The launch post got hundreds of likes.

The adoption graph was a flatline with a small bump on launch day. 1.4% of accounts had ever even opened it.

So we finally did what we should've done five months earlier: we got on calls with the exact customers who asked for it.

One of them, an ops manager, said something I still think about:

"Oh, I saw it. It's beautiful. I just don't have time to build a dashboard."

Then she told us what she actually did every Friday: export raw data into a spreadsheet, clean it up, and email a summary to her boss. Every. Single. Week.

She didn't want a dashboard. She wanted that hour of her life back.

Six weeks later, we shipped the least glamorous feature I've ever been part of: a button that said "Email me this report every Friday."

It became our most-adopted feature that year.

The lesson I keep coming back to:

**Users describe solutions, not problems.**

When someone says "we need a dashboard," they're handing you a spec for the solution they can imagine — not the problem they actually have. Our job is to ask "why?" until we find the job underneath.

Five months of engineering, or two weeks of watching customers work. Both would've found the Friday email. Only one of them cost us half a roadmap.

Now every feature request gets one question before anything else: "Walk me through what you were doing right before you decided you needed this."

The answers are almost never what the request said. They're always better.
