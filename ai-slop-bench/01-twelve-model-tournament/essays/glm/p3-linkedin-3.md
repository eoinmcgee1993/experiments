Six weeks of work. Shipped to 40,000 users.

Twelve people used it. Two of them were on our team.

Last year, our users kept asking for "more flexibility" in their dashboards. So we did what excited product teams do: we built a fully customizable dashboard builder. Drag and drop. Ten widget types. Custom color themes. The demo looked incredible.

We shipped it with a launch email, a blog post, and a lot of pride.

Thirty days later, the numbers came in:

→ 47 people opened the builder
→ 12 saved a custom dashboard
→ 0 came back a second week

I remember staring at the analytics, genuinely confused. The feature worked flawlessly. Almost no bugs. So where was everybody?

It took a few humbling user conversations to find out.

1. We heard "more flexibility" and translated it into "build everything." When we finally sat down with the users who asked, they wanted one thing: their two most important metrics on a single screen. We could have shipped that in a week.

2. We never defined success. No target, no baseline. We assumed adoption would just happen. "If we build it, they will come" isn't a strategy — it's a hope wearing a strategy costume.

3. We hid it. The entrance lived in a settings menu most users never open. The feature didn't fail on value; it failed on discovery. Placement is part of the product.

The lesson I still carry:

Adoption is designed, not assumed. Every feature has to clear three bars — it solves a real problem (validated, not guessed), people can find it, and it earns a second visit. We didn't deliberately clear any of them. We got lucky on none of them.

Now, before we build anything, three questions get answered in writing:

• What specific problem is this solving, and how do we know it's real?
• How will people discover it? (If the answer is "the changelog," we start over.)
• What number tells us it worked — and what do we do if it doesn't?

We also run "concierge tests" now: manually delivering a feature's value to a handful of users before writing any code. It's humbling how often the manual version reveals nobody wants the automated one.

By the way — that simple side-by-side view? We finally built it months later. One week of work. Four thousand users in the first month.

The dashboard builder still sits in our feature graveyard, and I'm oddly grateful. It cost six weeks and taught us more about building the right thing than any successful launch did.

Unused features aren't failures. They're tuition.

Just try not to overpay.
