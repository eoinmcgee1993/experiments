We spent three months building a feature nobody asked for.

It started the way these things always start. A few power users mentioned something adjacent in a support ticket. Someone sketched a mock in a meeting. Suddenly we had a Jira epic, a design sprint, and a Slack channel with 40 people in it.

The feature was beautiful. Edge cases handled. Analytics instrumented. Launch post drafted. We even named it.

Week one after ship: 12 users tried it.
Week two: 4.
Week four: 1, and I think it was me checking that it still worked.

Nobody complained. Nobody filed bugs. They just… didn’t care. The problem we solved was real for us. It was almost imaginary for them.

Here’s what I got wrong, and what I’d do differently.

I confused internal conviction with user demand. We were solving our own frustration with the product, then projecting it onto customers. “If we need this, they must need it too” is not research. It’s autobiography.

I treated discovery as a phase we had already finished. We talked to users—just not about this. We assumed adjacent pain meant this solution. We never put a prototype in front of five people and asked them to complete a real task. We never asked what they would stop doing if this existed. We never asked what they already used instead.

I optimized for completeness over learning. We built the full version because shipping “half” felt unprofessional. In reality, the unprofessional thing was spending a quarter on an untested bet. A weekend prototype and a dozen conversations would have killed it—or reshaped it—before we wrote production code.

The feature is gone now. We turned it off. The code is still in the repo like a monument to a meeting that went too well.

The lesson I keep: shipping is not validation. Usage is. Before the epic, write the smallest thing that could prove someone would miss it if it disappeared. If you can’t get that signal, you don’t have a feature. You have a story you told yourselves.

Talk to users about the job, not your idea. Watch what they already do. Build less, sooner. And if the room is excited and the customers are quiet—believe the quiet.
