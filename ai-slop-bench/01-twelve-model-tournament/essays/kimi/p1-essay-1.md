# Why Most Product Roadmaps Fail

Every product organization has a roadmap, and most of them are fiction. Not malicious fiction—aspirational fiction. They promise features on dates nobody can honestly guarantee, and within a quarter or two, reality has usually made other plans. The failure isn't in execution; it's baked into how roadmaps are conceived.

The deepest flaw is that roadmaps confuse outputs with outcomes. A typical roadmap is a list of features: a dashboard in Q2, an integration in Q3. But features are solutions to problems we haven't necessarily validated. Committing to a solution months in advance assumes we already know what will work—an assumption that survives contact with actual customers maybe half the time. Teams then ship to satisfy the document rather than solve the problem, becoming what product thinker John Cutler calls a "feature factory": busy, productive, and strategically irrelevant.

Worse, roadmaps are rarely products of evidence; they're products of negotiation. Sales needs a commitment to close a deal. An executive has a pet idea. A competitor launched something, so it goes on the list. The resulting artifact reflects internal power dynamics more than customer insight—which is why so many roadmap items die quietly after launch. They were never wanted by anyone outside the building.

Then there's the problem of time. Roadmaps imply a certainty about the future that doesn't exist. Software estimation is notoriously unreliable, and the further out a plan extends, the more it resembles astrology. Markets shift, assumptions break, learning accumulates—yet the roadmap, refreshed quarterly at best, sits frozen like a photograph of a moving scene. Teams keep marching toward stale priorities because updating the plan feels like admitting failure.

Underneath all of this usually lies a missing strategy. A roadmap without strategy is just a compromise document: something for every stakeholder, excellence for no one. Real strategy means choosing what *not* to do, and most roadmaps conspicuously avoid that choice.

The fix isn't to abandon roadmaps but to change what they are. Effective teams organize roadmaps around problems and outcomes rather than features and dates. They use horizons like *now / next / later* instead of false-precision timelines. They treat each item as a hypothesis to be tested, not a promise to be kept, and they revise continuously as they learn. In short, they treat the roadmap as a living statement of bets—humble about the future, ruthless about priorities, and accountable to results rather than to the plan itself.

A roadmap should be a compass, not a contract. Most fail because organizations insist on the opposite.
