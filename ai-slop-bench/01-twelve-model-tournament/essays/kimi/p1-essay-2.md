# Why Most Product Roadmaps Fail

Every year, product teams invest weeks crafting roadmaps — polished timelines of features stretching quarters into the future. And every year, most of those roadmaps quietly collapse: dates slip, priorities shuffle, entire initiatives vanish. The failure is so common it's practically a feature of the format. But roadmaps don't fail because teams are incompetent. They fail because of what organizations ask a roadmap to be.

The first problem is false certainty. A roadmap encodes predictions — what will be built, when it will ship — in a domain defined by uncertainty. Software estimates are notoriously unreliable; customer preferences shift; competitors move. Yet the roadmap's visual grammar, bars on a timeline and dates on a slide, communicates confidence. Stakeholders read a date as a promise, not a guess. When reality intrudes, the team faces a choice: miss the date and lose credibility, or hit it by shipping something rushed, gutted, or wrong. Either way, the roadmap has failed at its real job — creating trust.

Second, most roadmaps describe outputs, not outcomes. They list features ("build a dashboard") rather than problems ("customers can't see their usage, so they churn"). This matters because features are guesses about solutions, and many guesses are wrong. A team locked into delivering a feature will ship it even after learning it won't work. The roadmap becomes a conveyor belt — a "feature factory," in John Cutler's phrase — where success is measured by shipping, not by whether anything improved.

Third, roadmaps are political artifacts. The roadmap is where sales, executives, and engineering fight their proxy wars. A salesperson promises a feature to close a deal; an executive inserts a pet project; the loudest voice wins the quarter. The resulting document reflects organizational power, not customer evidence or strategy. Without a coherent strategy beneath it, the roadmap is a negotiated settlement — and negotiated settlements rarely produce great products.

Finally, roadmaps harden. Once published, they develop constituencies. Changing them feels like admitting failure, so teams stop updating them even as they learn. The plan, built on last quarter's assumptions, outlives its usefulness. As Eisenhower observed, plans are worthless but planning is everything: the roadmap's real value lies in the alignment that produces it, yet organizations worship the artifact and discard the practice.

The fix isn't to abandon roadmaps but to hold them differently: express them as outcomes rather than features, organize by confidence — now, next, later — rather than by date, and treat every item as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a commitment to be honored. Roadmaps fail when they're used as contracts. They succeed when they're used as compasses: directional, revisable, and honest about the fog ahead.
