We spent 4 months building a feature that 11 people used.

Not 11%. Eleven humans.

The feature was a custom report builder for our analytics product. Customers had asked for it — in surveys, in support tickets, on sales calls. "We need more flexible reporting." So we listened. Or we thought we did.

We scoped it. Designed it. Built it. Polished it. Shipped it with a launch email and an in-app tour.

Crickets.

For weeks I checked the usage dashboard every morning, half-hoping the data was wrong. It wasn't.

Here's what actually went wrong — and it took me an embarrassingly long time to see it:

We heard the words "we want flexible reporting" and built *our interpretation* of that sentence. We never asked what people were trying to do when they said it.

Turns out, customers didn't want to build reports. They wanted an answer to one specific question: "Which campaigns drove revenue last month?" Ideally in one click. Better yet, pushed to them every Monday morning.

We built a workshop. They wanted a vending machine.

The lesson that stuck with me:

👉 Customers describe solutions. Your job is to excavate the problem underneath.

When someone requests a feature, the most valuable follow-up isn't "what should it do?" It's "walk me through the last time you needed this."

That single question has saved my team months of wasted work since. Sometimes it leads to building something smaller. Sometimes it means building nothing at all — because the real pain lives somewhere else entirely.

The epilogue: we eventually shipped a simple "Top campaigns by revenue" card on the main dashboard. It took three days to build. It's now one of the most-viewed elements in the product.

Three days of work, aimed at the actual problem, beat four months of work aimed at the stated request.

I still think about those 11 users sometimes. (Thank you for trying it. You deserved better.)

If you're about to kick off a big build, try this before writing a single line of code: ask five customers to describe the last time they felt the pain you're solving. Watch what they *do*, not just what they say.

You'll either build it better — or realize you should build something else entirely.
