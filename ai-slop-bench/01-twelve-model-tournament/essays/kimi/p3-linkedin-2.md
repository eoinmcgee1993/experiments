We spent 4 months building a feature nobody wanted.

Total users after launch: 3. Two of them were on my team.

The feature was genuinely good. Clean code, elegant UI, solid documentation. We debated edge cases in design reviews for weeks. On launch day, we posted the announcement, watched the dashboards, and waited.

Nothing.

Not angry feedback. Not bug reports. Just silence. And silence is worse — at least complaints mean someone cared enough to try.

Here's the uncomfortable truth I had to sit with:

We never asked a single customer if they wanted it.

The idea came from an internal brainstorm. It sounded smart in the room. Everyone nodded. We confused "we would use this" with "our users need this." Classic build trap.

When I finally called customers to ask why they hadn't touched it, the answers were almost embarrassing:

→ "I didn't know it existed."
→ "We already solve that another way."
→ "Honestly, that's not a problem for us."

Months of engineering time. The lesson cost us a quarter — but it's saved us many since.

Now, before anything makes it onto the roadmap, we do three things:

1. Talk to 5–10 users about the *problem*, not the solution. If they don't bring up the problem unprompted, it probably isn't painful enough to build for.

2. Test demand before writing code. A fake-door button, a landing page, a manual version run by a human. Interest first, code second.

3. Define what "used" means before launch. If we can't name the adoption metric that would justify keeping the feature, we're not ready to build it.

The feature itself? We sunset it six months later. Deleting it stung more than building it.

But here's the reframe I carry with me:

Shipping something nobody uses isn't failure. Shipping it *without learning anything* is.

Your users will tell you exactly what to build — if you ask before you build, not after.
