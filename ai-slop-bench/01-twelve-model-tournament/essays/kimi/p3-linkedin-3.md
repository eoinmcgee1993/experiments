We spent 4 months building a feature that 11 people used.

Not 11 percent. Eleven people. Total.

The feature was customizable reporting dashboards. Sales kept hearing "we need better reporting" on calls. It showed up in every churn survey. The roadmap committee greenlit it fast — this was clearly what customers wanted.

We did everything "right." Specced it carefully. Built it well. Launched with in-app announcements, email campaigns, even a webinar.

Week one: a few hundred clicks. Mostly curiosity.
Month three: 11 weekly active users. Out of 40,000 accounts.

The post-mortem was uncomfortable. Because here's the truth: customers weren't lying when they said they wanted better reporting. They did want it — in the abstract. But wanting a feature and using a feature are separated by a canyon of friction: setup time, learning curve, breaking an existing habit.

What they actually wanted wasn't "customizable dashboards." It was the answer to one specific question their boss asks every Monday. We handed them a toolkit. They wanted an answer.

The lesson I carry with me now:

**"Customers asked for it" is not validation. It's a hypothesis.**

Saying yes to a feature costs a customer nothing. Using it costs them time, attention, and behavior change. The gap between stated preference and revealed preference is where roadmaps go to die.

What I do differently today:

1. **Before building, I ask: what do users do *today* to solve this?** If the answer is "nothing" or "they just live with it," that's a warning sign, not an opportunity.

2. **I test with the cheapest possible version.** A manual workaround. A spreadsheet. A fake door. If people won't use the clunky version, they won't use the polished one either.

3. **When someone requests a feature, I dig for the problem underneath.** "I need better reporting" becomes "Tell me about the last time reporting failed you." The story is always more useful than the request.

And those 11 users? They love the feature. Deeply. It's core to their workflow. We just built a power tool for a niche while believing we were building a staple for everyone.

That distinction — who it's actually for, and how many of them exist — is worth more than any roadmap meeting I've ever sat in.

The most expensive code isn't the code that breaks. It's the code that runs perfectly, serving no one.
