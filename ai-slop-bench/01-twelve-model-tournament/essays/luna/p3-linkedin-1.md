We once shipped a feature we were sure customers would love.

It checked all the boxes:

- The problem sounded real.
- The solution was technically elegant.
- Stakeholders supported it.
- The roadmap had room for it.
- The launch went smoothly.

And then… almost nobody used it.

At first, we blamed discoverability. So we improved the navigation, added tooltips, and mentioned it in release notes.

Usage barely moved.

Then we blamed onboarding. We created documentation, examples, and an email campaign.

Still, almost no adoption.

The uncomfortable truth was simpler: we had built something people didn’t need badly enough to change their behavior.

That experience changed how I think about product development.

A feature can be well-designed, well-engineered, and well-marketed—and still fail if it solves a low-priority problem.

The biggest lesson wasn’t “validate more.” It was to validate the *right thing* before writing much code:

1. **How often does this problem occur?**  
2. **What are people doing today instead?**  
3. **What does the problem cost them?**  
4. **Who is actively asking for a solution?**  
5. **What would they stop doing if this existed?**

That last question is especially important. Adoption usually requires a behavior change. If the current workaround is “good enough,” even a better product may not be enough.

Now, before committing to a feature, we try to find evidence of urgency—not just agreement. We look for repeated pain in support conversations, workarounds in user behavior, requests from multiple customers, or measurable business impact.

A polite “That sounds useful” is not the same as someone saying, “When can I use it?”

Shipping unused work is frustrating, but it isn’t wasted if it improves the team’s judgment.

The goal isn’t to predict perfectly.

It’s to learn earlier, spend less, and build more of what people are already trying to solve.

Sometimes the most valuable feature is the one you decide not to ship.
