We once shipped a feature we were convinced customers needed.

The research looked promising. The roadmap discussion was enthusiastic. The team spent weeks designing, building, testing, and launching it.

Then… almost nobody used it.

At first, we blamed discoverability.

So we added tooltips, announcements, onboarding prompts, and documentation.

Usage barely moved.

That’s when we realized the problem wasn’t that customers couldn’t find the feature.

They didn’t have a strong enough reason to use it.

The feature solved a problem we thought was important—not a problem customers were actively trying to solve.

The lesson wasn’t “validate your ideas” in the abstract. We already knew that.

The real lesson was to validate *behavior*, not opinions.

People will often say a feature sounds useful. They may even say they would use it. But that doesn’t mean the problem is painful enough to change their workflow, spend money, or overcome the friction of adopting something new.

Today, before investing heavily in a feature, we try to answer a few more practical questions:

- How are customers solving this problem today?
- How frequently does it happen?
- What does the current workaround cost them?
- What would they stop doing if our solution existed?
- Can we observe them taking action—not just expressing interest?

Sometimes the best validation is not a survey or a roadmap vote. It’s a manual process, a prototype, a waitlist, or a customer willing to try an imperfect solution immediately.

Shipping a feature nobody used was disappointing.

But it was also useful. It reminded us that building something well is not the same as building something valuable.

A successful launch isn’t measured by how proud the team feels when the feature goes live.

It’s measured by whether the feature meaningfully changes what customers do.
