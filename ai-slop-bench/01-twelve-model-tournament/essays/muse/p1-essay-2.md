**Why Most Product Roadmaps Fail**

Most product roadmaps fail not because product managers can't plan, but because we ask roadmaps to do the impossible. We want them to inspire vision, align executives, satisfy sales, promise customers certainty, and guide engineers — all at once. Stretched across these conflicting jobs, the roadmap snaps. It stops being a tool for strategy and becomes theatre: a pretty timeline that everyone nods at and nobody trusts.

First, most roadmaps confuse outputs with outcomes. They list features and dates — "mobile app in Q2, AI chatbot in Q3" — instead of the problems to solve or the impact to create. This turns product into a feature factory. Teams are measured on shipping on time, not on whether anything got better for users or the business. When a date slips, or a feature flops, the roadmap looks like a failure, even if the team learned something valuable.

Second, they lack strategy. A great roadmap should be a visual argument: this is where we're playing, this is how we'll win, and therefore this is what matters now. Too many are just aggregated wish lists — a little for Sales, a little for the CEO, a little for the biggest customer. Without ruthless prioritization and explicit trade-offs, everything is P0, which means nothing is. The roadmap tries to keep peace rather than make choices, and so it pleases no one for long.

Third, they pretend uncertainty doesn't exist. Products live in a world of shifting customers, competitors, and technical surprises. Yet roadmaps are often carved in stone for 12-18 months, built on untested assumptions and no discovery. There's no room to learn, pivot, or kill bad ideas. Teams either follow the plan blindly off a cliff, or constantly replan in panic. In both cases, trust erodes.

The roadmaps that succeed do the opposite. They start with outcomes — reduce churn by 10%, expand into mid-market — and hold dates loosely and learning tightly. They make strategy explicit, show what they're *not* doing, and get updated as evidence comes in. Most importantly, they're treated as communication tools for alignment, not contracts for delivery.

A roadmap doesn't fail when a date changes. It fails when it stops helping a team decide what matters next.
