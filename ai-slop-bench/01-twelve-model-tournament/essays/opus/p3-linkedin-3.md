We spent four months building a feature that 11 people used.

Not 11%. Eleven people. Out of roughly 40,000 monthly active accounts.

It was a custom dashboard builder. Drag-and-drop widgets, saved views, shareable links. The whole thing. And we had *evidence* it was wanted: 60+ support tickets asking for better reporting, three enterprise deals where it came up on the call, and a customer advisory board that lit up when we showed the mockups.

Everyone said they'd use it. Almost nobody did.

Here's what I got wrong, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to see it.

**I confirmed the problem, then invented the solution.**

The tickets were real. People genuinely couldn't get the numbers they needed. But "I can't see my data" doesn't mean "give me a canvas and let me build my own dashboard." When we finally went back and watched what those 60 requesters actually did, most of them wanted one specific number, once a week, in an email. They didn't want a tool. They wanted an answer.

We built a workshop for people who wanted a finished piece of furniture.

**I mistook enthusiasm for intent.**

"That looks great" is the cheapest sentence in software. It costs a customer nothing to be encouraging on a call, and it costs them a lot to change how their team works on a Monday morning. I never asked the uncomfortable follow-up: *What are you doing today instead? What would you stop doing to make room for this?* If someone can't tell you what this replaces, they probably won't adopt it.

**I let the loudest accounts stand in for the market.**

The advisory board loved it because the advisory board is full of power users — the 2% who want knobs and levers. They're a terrible proxy for the median customer, who logs in twice a week and wants to leave.

What changed after that:

We now write down the adoption number we expect *before* we build. Not a vague "this will be popular" — an actual figure, with the reasoning behind it. When we're wrong, we can see exactly which assumption broke. Half the time, writing the number down kills the idea on the spot.

We also ship the ugly version first. For the reporting problem, the fix that finally worked was a weekly email with five metrics and no configuration at all. It took nine days. Adoption is north of 30%.

The dashboard builder is still in the product. It has a small, loyal following, and I'm genuinely glad those users have it. But it cost four months to learn something a two-week experiment would have taught us.

The most expensive thing you can build is a solution to a problem you never fully understood.

If you've shipped something into silence, I'd love to hear what it taught you. I suspect that's where most of the real learning in this job actually lives.
