We once shipped a feature we were convinced customers would love.

The idea was solid.  
The roadmap said it mattered.  
The team spent weeks designing, building, testing, and polishing it.

Then we launched.

Almost nobody used it.

Our first instinct was to blame discoverability. So we added onboarding prompts, updated documentation, and highlighted the feature in customer emails.

Usage barely moved.

The uncomfortable truth: we had validated that customers understood the problem—but not that the problem was important enough for them to change their behavior.

We had mistaken polite interest for genuine demand.

That experience changed how I think about product development.

Now, before committing significant time to a feature, I look for stronger signals:

- Are customers already using a workaround?
- How frequently does the problem occur?
- What does the problem cost them today?
- Will they commit time, data, money, or reputation to a proposed solution?
- Can we test the behavior before building the full product?

Interviews still matter, but behavior is usually more reliable than enthusiasm.

A customer saying, “I’d use that,” is useful feedback.

A customer asking, “When can I have it?” is better.

A customer changing their workflow—or paying—to get it is better still.

Shipping an unused feature wasn’t a failure because the idea was wrong. It was a failure because we learned too late.

The goal isn’t to avoid every bad bet. That’s impossible.

The goal is to make bets smaller, test assumptions earlier, and treat evidence—not excitement—as the signal to scale.

Sometimes the most valuable feature you build is the one that teaches you how not to build the next one.
