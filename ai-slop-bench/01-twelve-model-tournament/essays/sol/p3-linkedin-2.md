We shipped a feature nobody used.

The team had spent weeks designing it, building it, polishing edge cases, and preparing the launch. Internally, everyone agreed it was valuable.

Customers disagreed—mostly by ignoring it.

At first, we blamed discoverability. We changed the placement, added tooltips, and mentioned it in onboarding.

Usage barely moved.

The uncomfortable truth: we had validated the problem, but not the urgency.

Customers agreed the feature sounded useful. But “useful” wasn’t enough to change their behavior. The problem wasn’t frequent, painful, or important enough to earn a place in their workflow.

That experience changed how I think about product development.

Before investing heavily, I now try to answer:

- When did the customer last experience this problem?
- What are they doing today to solve it?
- What does that workaround cost them?
- Would they change their behavior—or pay—to make the problem disappear?
- What is the smallest experiment that could test real demand?

Positive feedback can be misleading. People are generous with opinions, especially when saying yes costs nothing.

Behavior is stronger evidence.

A waitlist is stronger than enthusiasm.  
A prototype test is stronger than a survey.  
A manual pilot is stronger than a feature request.  
Repeated use is stronger than all of them.

The feature wasn’t a complete failure. It gave us something more valuable than adoption: a better standard for deciding what to build.

Don’t just validate that a problem exists.

Validate that it matters enough for someone to act.
