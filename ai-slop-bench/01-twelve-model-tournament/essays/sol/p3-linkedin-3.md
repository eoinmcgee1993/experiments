We shipped a feature nobody used.

Not “low adoption.”  
Not “slower than expected.”

Almost nobody touched it.

The team had spent weeks designing, building, testing, and polishing it. We believed the feature solved an obvious customer problem—and once we launched, we waited for the usage data to prove us right.

It didn’t.

Our first instinct was to improve onboarding. Maybe users couldn’t find it. Maybe the messaging wasn’t clear. Maybe we needed another tooltip.

But after speaking with customers, the real issue became obvious:

We had validated the problem, but not the priority.

Yes, customers experienced the problem. They simply didn’t experience it often enough—or feel it was painful enough—to change their behavior.

That distinction taught me an important lesson:

**A real problem is not automatically a problem worth building for.**

Before writing code, we should have asked:

- How are customers solving this today?
- How frequently does the problem occur?
- What does it cost them?
- What would they stop doing to use our solution?
- Have they actively looked for an alternative?

Complaints can be useful signals, but they aren’t commitments. Interest isn’t intent. And “I’d use that” is very different from changing an established workflow.

The feature wasn’t a total waste. It improved how we approach product discovery.

Now, we look for evidence of urgency—not just agreement. We test behavior before building the full solution. And we define what adoption should look like before launch, not after.

Shipping the wrong feature taught us more than shipping an easy win would have.

The goal isn’t to build what customers say sounds useful.

It’s to understand what matters enough for them to act.
