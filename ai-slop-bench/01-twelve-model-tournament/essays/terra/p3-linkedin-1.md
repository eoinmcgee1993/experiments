We shipped a feature nobody used.

Not “low adoption.”  
Not “needs more time.”  
Nobody used it.

We had spent weeks building it: designs, edge cases, QA, release notes, internal excitement. It solved a problem we believed customers had.

The problem? We believed more than we listened.

We had seen a few support requests and assumed they represented a broad need. We jumped quickly from signal to solution.

What we didn’t do was ask the harder questions:

- How often does this problem occur?
- Who experiences it most?
- What are they doing instead today?
- Is the current workaround actually painful enough to change behavior?
- Would they use this solution if it existed?

After launch, the data was clear. Customers weren’t ignoring the feature because it was hard to find. They were ignoring it because it wasn’t important.

That was an expensive lesson—but a valuable one.

Since then, I’ve tried to treat every feature idea as a hypothesis, not a commitment.

Before building, we now aim to validate three things:

1. **The problem is real** — not just anecdotal.  
2. **The problem is painful** — not merely inconvenient.  
3. **Our solution changes behavior** — not just sounds good in a roadmap meeting.  

Sometimes that means interviews. Sometimes it means a prototype. Sometimes it means a landing page, a manual workflow, or simply asking customers to choose between competing problems.

The goal isn’t to eliminate every failed feature.

The goal is to fail earlier, cheaper, and with more learning.

Shipping something nobody uses feels disappointing. But it’s also a reminder:

Output is not impact.  
A released feature is not a successful feature.  
And building faster is only useful when we’re building the right thing.
