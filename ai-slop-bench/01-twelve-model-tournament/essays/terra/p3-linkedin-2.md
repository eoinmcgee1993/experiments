We shipped a feature nobody used.

Not “low adoption.”  
Not “needs more awareness.”  
Nobody used it.

At first, we blamed the usual suspects:

- The onboarding wasn’t clear enough  
- Marketing didn’t promote it  
- Users needed more time  
- Maybe it was ahead of the market  

But the real answer was simpler: we solved a problem people didn’t care enough about.

The feature had come from a reasonable place. A few customer comments. Internal enthusiasm. A competitor had something similar. On paper, it made sense.

What we skipped was the hard question:

**“What are users doing today when this problem happens—and how painful is that workaround?”**

When we finally asked, the answer was revealing. Most users had a simple workaround that took less than a minute. A few didn’t experience the problem at all. And the people who did care wanted a different solution than the one we built.

We had validated the idea. We hadn’t validated the behavior.

That experience changed how I think about shipping.

Now, before building, I try to look for evidence of urgency:

- Are people already spending time or money to solve this?
- Do they describe the problem in their own words, without prompting?
- Is the workaround frustrating enough that they would change behavior?
- Can we test the value with a prototype, manual process, or lightweight experiment first?
- What would success look like in actual usage—not just positive feedback?

A feature launch is not proof of value. Usage is.

The good news: unused features are expensive lessons, but valuable ones. They teach humility. They expose assumptions. And, if you pay attention, they improve the next decision.

The goal isn’t to never ship something that misses.

The goal is to learn why it missed before you spend months building the next one.
