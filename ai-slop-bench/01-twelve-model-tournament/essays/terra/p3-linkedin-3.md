We shipped a feature nobody used.

Not “low adoption.” Not “needs better onboarding.”

Nobody used it.

The team had spent weeks building it. The request had come up in customer conversations. It looked valuable in planning. The design was polished, the implementation was solid, and we announced it with confidence.

Then we watched the data.

Silence.

Our first instinct was to fix the feature:

- Improve the UI  
- Add tooltips  
- Send an email announcement  
- Put it in the product tour  

But the real issue wasn’t discoverability.

It was relevance.

We had heard customers describe a problem and immediately translated it into a solution. We never slowed down to understand:

- How often the problem actually occurred  
- What they did instead  
- Whether it was painful enough to change behavior  
- Who specifically needed the solution  
- What tradeoff they would make to use it  

The feature solved a problem in theory, but not one important enough in practice.

The lesson: customer requests are not product requirements.

A request is evidence. It’s a starting point for discovery—not a roadmap item.

Now, before committing to a feature, we ask a different set of questions:

1. What job is the customer trying to get done?  
2. What is their current workaround?  
3. What does that workaround cost them?  
4. How many customers have this problem, and how often?  
5. What is the smallest test we can run before building?  

Sometimes the best outcome of product discovery is deciding not to build.

That can feel disappointing when a team is eager to ship. But avoiding months of work on something customers won’t adopt is a win—not a failure.

Shipping teaches you what works.

But listening before shipping teaches you what’s worth building.
